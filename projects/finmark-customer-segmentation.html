FinMark customer cleaning & segmentation

Three linked datasets from a practice financial-services company (customers, products and transactions) arrived with duplicates, gaps and impossible values. I cleaned and tested them in Python, then segmented customers with K-Means and confirmed the segments with hierarchical clustering.

01Business problem

FinMark wanted to know which of its customers behave alike, so it could stop treating them all the same. The answer depended on joining feedback to transactions, and neither file was usable as delivered. Both had duplicate rows and missing values, and the satisfaction scores included numbers that could not exist on a 1–10 scale.

02Objectives

  • Prepare the customers, products and transactions datasets for analysis.
  • Explore how satisfaction, likelihood to recommend, products and transactions behave.
  • Segment customers by transaction amount and satisfaction score, and recommend an action for each segment.
  • Check whether a second clustering method agrees with the first.

04Data preparation

  1. De-duplicate50 transaction, 5 product and 81 feedback duplicates dropped.
  2. Test distributionsShapiro-Wilk: neither transaction amount nor satisfaction score is normal (p < 0.05).
  3. Flag outliers (IQR)10 satisfaction scores between 51 and 60 on a 1–10 scale set to missing.
  4. ImputeMissing amounts and scores filled with the median (under 5% missing); an all-empty age column dropped.
  5. Fix typesIDs to text, amounts and scores to numbers, dates to datetime; year and month extracted.
  6. Engineer & scaleSatisfaction, recommendation and engagement categories built; StandardScaler applied before clustering.

Before

  • Duplicates in all three datasets.
  • 100 missing transaction amounts, 101 missing satisfaction scores.
  • Scores of 51–60 on a 1–10 scale.
  • Dates and IDs stored as the wrong types.

After

  • No duplicate rows.
  • No missing values in the fields used for clustering.
  • Impossible scores removed before imputation.
  • Correct types, with date parts and engagement features added.

05Analytical approach

  • Exploration: histograms, count plots, box plots, a risk-by-income heatmap and monthly trends.
  • Choosing k: the elbow method on within-cluster sum of squares (k = 1 to 10) pointed to three clusters.
  • K-Means (k = 3) on satisfaction score and transaction amount.
  • Cross-check: agglomerative hierarchical clustering with Ward linkage and a dendrogram, also at three clusters.

06Findings

  • Three segments: low satisfaction and low transactions; moderate on both; and high satisfaction with high transactions.
  • Both methods agree. Hierarchical clustering produced similar segments, which supports the K-Means result.
  • Satisfaction does not predict recommendation here. The correlation between satisfaction score and likelihood to recommend is −0.02.
  • Products track risk to income. Low-risk products target low-income groups, and high-risk products target high-income groups.

07Recommendations

From the analysis

  • Low / low: feedback surveys to find pain points, plus loyalty incentives.
  • Moderate: tiered rewards and targeted promotions to move them up.
  • High / high: VIP treatment, early access and priority service to keep them.

Limits to keep in mind

  • Practice data: the segments describe this dataset, not real customers.
  • Only two features were clustered; product preference and frequency were left for further work.
